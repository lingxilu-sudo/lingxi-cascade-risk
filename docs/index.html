Coupling orbital mechanics with actuarial science for satellite insurance pricing

1-min summary ↓ Download full paper (PDF)

Problem

Space insurance prices risk using historical failure rates, ignoring cascade feedback. One collision can trigger chain reactions, creating correlated losses across entire portfolios.

Method

Physics-based Monte Carlo simulation (1,000 paths, 50 years) coupled with a Solvency-II-style premium decomposition. Fragment yields are an event-anchored prior, checked against three documented collisions.

Key Finding

Cascade-adjusted premiums are 16.5× higher than a 0.5%-of-hull in-orbit failure benchmark ($2,058M vs $125M per year for 500 satellites). That $1,933M gap is a solvency signal for a concentrated portfolio, not a literal market-wide forecast.

01

Executive Overview

Space insurance is a $1.2 billion market. The way we price it hasn't changed in decades: look at what broke last year, add a margin, move on.

There's a problem with this. It assumes tomorrow will look like yesterday. But Low Earth Orbit doesn't work that way. One collision makes hundreds of fragments. Each fragment is a projectile. Those projectiles hit other things, making more fragments. The whole system has a feedback loop that nobody's pricing in.

What is Kessler Syndrome?

First described by NASA scientist Donald Kessler in 1978, the syndrome predicts that once debris density exceeds a critical threshold, collisions become self-sustaining: each impact generates more debris than natural decay removes, leading to exponential growth. In the worst case, entire orbital shells become unusable — a scenario sometimes called "cascading collisional breakup."

This framework couples physics-based cascade simulation with actuarial pricing. The result: insurance premiums 16.5 times higher than a 0.5%-of-hull in-orbit failure benchmark — a gap that points to both a solvency risk for concentrated portfolios and a market failure in orbital sustainability. The 16.5× is conditional: it assigns the whole >10 cm catalogue to one shell, uses expected claim counts, treats debris–debris fragments as catalogue objects (an upper bound in that dimension), and sits close to a finite-time singularity at 61 years.

Why This Matters

If insurers underprice risk by 16.5×, operators have no economic incentive to choose less congested orbits or invest in debris mitigation. Correct pricing aligns private costs with systemic risks — making sustainability a market force rather than a regulatory burden.

02

Physical Layer: Collision-Driven Debris Dynamics

I'm looking at one shell of LEO, centered at 800 km altitude, about 200 km thick (700–900 km). That's where most of the dead satellites are. That's also where Starlink and everyone else wants to operate.

The cascade rate is not tuned. Collision terms come from the Kessler–Flournoy flux equation, so debris growth is what the physics says it should be. That is not the same as "no free parameters": the initial population N0, the yield parameters, the 0.5% benchmark and the portfolio size are modelling choices, and their influence is quantified below.

Collision Rate: The Kessler–Flournoy Formula

The expected annual collision rate between debris and satellites:

R = Nd · Ns · σ · v · T / V

At current population levels, this yields ~7 debris–satellite and ~16 debris–debris collisions per year.

The Quadratic Feedback

Debris–debris collision rate scales with Nd² — doubling the debris quadruples the collision rate. Each collision produces new fragments (event-anchored log-normal yields; the NASA Standard Breakup Model is an order-of-magnitude anchor only), which further accelerate collisions. This is the mathematical root of the cascade: a self-reinforcing loop with no natural brake.

03

Consistency Check: Anchored in Reality

Before the model projects future risk, its fragment yields should be plausible against known events. The three documented collisions below created roughly 7,300 catalogued fragments in total. They are used as an order-of-magnitude check, not to fit the operative log-normal parameters:

January 11, 2007
Fengyun-1C Anti-Satellite Test
China destroyed its own weather satellite with a ground-launched missile. The 880 kg spacecraft at 865 km altitude generated ~3,500 fragments (>10 cm) — the largest single debris creation event in history. Many fragments remain in orbit today.
February 10, 2009
Iridium 33 × Cosmos 2251
The first accidental hypervelocity collision between two intact satellites. An active Iridium communications satellite (560 kg) struck a defunct Russian military satellite (950 kg) at 11.7 km/s over northern Siberia, generating ~2,300 fragments (>10 cm) and a combined 1,510 kg of debris.
November 15, 2021
Kosmos 1408 Anti-Satellite Test
Russia conducted a direct-ascent ASAT test against its own defunct reconnaissance satellite, creating ~1,500 fragments (>10 cm) from 2,200 kg of mass at 485 km. The ISS crew took shelter twice as debris passed within close proximity.

These events anchor the order of magnitude of fragment yields: debris–satellite collisions produce a median of ~245 fragments (log-normal, μ=5.5, σ=0.8), while debris–debris collisions produce a median of ~3 fragments (log-normal, μ=1.1, σ=0.5). The counts above are catalogue totals rather than per-collision yields, so the operative parameters are an event-anchored prior, not a fitted calibration; a formal fit to fragments per kilogram gives a median of about 64, which differs from the operative 245. The asymmetry reflects the mass differential: a fragment hitting a satellite releases far more energy than two small fragments colliding.

04

Economic Layer: From Debris to Dollars

The physics layer outputs debris population trajectories — how many objects occupy the shell at each point in time. To convert this into financial risk, we need a bridge: collision physics to loss probabilities to insurance premiums.

The Conversion Chain

Step 1: From debris count N(t), compute each satellite's annual hit probability using the same flux equation: phit = Nd × σ × v × T / V. At current debris levels, this is ~0.09% per satellite per year — small, but non-negligible across a portfolio.

Step 2: Multiply hit probability by satellite value ($50M average) to get per-policy expected loss. Sum across the 500-satellite insured portfolio: expected loss is correlation-invariant. A separate Gaussian-copula module (ρ = 0.3) is used for variance and tail analysis only and does not enter the headline premium.

Step 3: From the loss distribution across 1,000 Monte Carlo paths, extract risk metrics (VaR, CVaR, EVT) and price using a Solvency-II-style framework.

Premium Decomposition (Solvency-II-style)

This stylised decomposition splits the premium into four parts — it is a pricing model, not the calculation prescribed by the Solvency II Directive:

ComponentDescriptionAmount
Expected Loss (EL)Mean worst-year portfolio loss$921.4M

Capital Charge4% cost of VaR₉₉ regulatory capital$32.0M
Expense Loading15% admin costs + profit margin$138.2M
Total Premium$2,058M / yr

The risk loading ($966M) is the biggest piece — bigger than the expected loss itself. That's because the tail is severe: in the worst 5% of scenarios, average losses reach $1,565M, about 70% above the mean.

Time alignment. This is the fair premium at the end of the horizon, not today's. The worst year is the final step in 100% of paths, so the ratio measures how a static benchmark falls behind a growing cascade by year 50. The same model gives expected losses of $22.3M at year 0 (0.18× the benchmark) and $111.0M at year 25 (0.89×). The benchmark covers all in-orbit failures while the model prices debris collisions only, so the ratios are conservative on coverage.

Systemic Risk Warning

That $1,933M gap between the 0.5% benchmark ($125M) and the cascade-adjusted premium ($2,058M) is a solvency signal. It exceeds the roughly $1.2B annual global premium base because the test portfolio puts 500 high-value satellites in a single congested shell. If a cascade event hit while cover was priced off historical failure rates, claims on such a concentrated book could outrun reserves.

Market Design Implication

But here's the flip side: if we price cascade risk correctly, we get economic incentives for sustainability for free. Operators who put satellites in less crowded shells or invest in end-of-life deorbiting would get lower premiums — making debris mitigation profitable without needing regulators to force it.

06

Methodology & Limitations

Simulation Architecture

Step 1 — Collision-driven growth. Debris evolution is governed by Poisson-distributed collision events at each time step (Δt = 0.1 yr). Fragment yields follow event-anchored log-normal distributions; the cascade rate itself has no separately tuned parameter.

Step 2 — Monte Carlo ensemble. 1,000 independent 50-year paths produce a full probability distribution of debris trajectories and corresponding losses.

Step 3 — Risk quantification. VaR, CVaR, and EVT (Generalized Pareto Distribution, with the exceedance ratio ζ) metrics are extracted from the worst-year loss distribution.

Step 4 — Premium calculation. Solvency-II-style decomposition on the worst-year loss distribution; correlation enters only through a separate Gaussian-copula module used for variance and tail analysis.

Limitations

Single-shell approximation. The model represents only the 800 km shell. In reality, debris migrates across altitudes due to atmospheric drag and collision-induced velocity changes. A multi-shell model would capture cross-altitude contamination effects.

Static populations. Both the satellite count (8,000) and debris injection rate are held constant. Dynamic modeling of mega-constellation deployment schedules and retirement would improve realism.

No active debris removal. The model does not include ADR missions, which could shift the debris trajectory below the cascade threshold.

Simplified fragment physics. The operative log-normal yield is an event-anchored prior, not a fitted calibration. Collision mass and energy do not enter the sampler, and the NASA Standard Breakup Model is retained only as an order-of-magnitude anchor.

A generous debris–debris term. The model tracks catalogue-scale objects (>10 cm), yet each debris–debris event adds its ~3 fragments to that count, and both channels share the 10 m² satellite cross-section. Both choices raise the loss: excluding the DD fragments lowers expected loss from $921M to $463M; using a 1 m² DD section lowers it to $484M. The 16.5× is an upper bound in this dimension.

Single-shell population accounting. The model assigns the entire global >10 cm catalogue (36,500 objects) to the 700–900 km shell, whereas only part of it resides there. A sensitivity check over N0 = 8,000 to 36,500 moves the expected loss from $114M to $925M; the headline should be read as an upper-bound-concentrated case.

Proximity to a finite-time singularity. The mean-field backbone diverges at t* ≈ 61 years, and the 50-year horizon sits at 82% of t*; N(55) = 89×N0 and N(60) = 582×N0. Small parameter changes move t* between roughly 27 and 136 years, so late-horizon losses are sensitive to the yield scale and decay rate.

Expected claim counts. The loss bridge uses phit × Npol rather than sampling binomial claim counts. A binomial check raises expected loss by about 35% ($1,243M vs $921M) and VaR₉₅ by about 28% ($1,753M vs $1,365M), so the headline understates rather than overstates losses under claim-count noise.

Benchmark sensitivity. The 0.5%-of-hull benchmark is an assumption, not an observed premium. At 0.3% the multiplier is 27.5×; at 1.0% it is 8.2×. The direction of the gap is robust; its size is not.

Discretization. A convergence check at 300 paths (seed 42) gives EL of $896.5M at Δt = 0.2, $925.0M at 0.1, and $913.0M at 0.05; the 0.1-year step is within about 1.3% of the finest step, while VaR₉₅ is stable within 0.2%. The step is adequate for the reported precision.

Planned Extensions

Multi-shell model with cross-altitude migration; NORAD TLE data integration for empirical debris tracking; dynamic constellation deployment modeling; reinsurance layer pricing (excess-of-loss, stop-loss structures); and sensitivity analysis using Sobol indices to identify the most impactful parameters.
